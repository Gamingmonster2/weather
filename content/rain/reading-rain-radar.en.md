# How to read rain radar like a forecaster

Radar is the fastest way to answer a simple question: **is rain actually coming, and when?** Rain.ly overlays RainViewer composite radar on an OpenStreetMap base so you can see both the big picture and the street-level context.

## What the colours mean

Radar reflectivity is usually painted from cool to hot colours. As a practical rule of thumb:

- **Light blue / green** — drizzle or light rain, often under 2 mm/h.
- **Yellow / orange** — moderate showers, the kind that soaks roads quickly.
- **Red / magenta** — heavy rain or convective cores; expect poor visibility and ponding.
- **Very intense cores** inside a larger system can indicate hail or thunderstorm downpours.

> Colour scales differ between providers. Treat them as relative intensity, not as an exact rainfall gauge.

## Past frames vs nowcast

The timeline mixes **past scans** (what the radar actually observed) with a short **nowcast** (an extrapolation of recent motion). Past frames are observations; nowcast frames are guidance for roughly the next 30–90 minutes and become less reliable the further out you go.

## Reading movement

1. Play the last few frames and note the direction of travel.
2. Look for growth or decay — brightening blobs mean intensification, fading means the shower is weakening.
3. Along the Libyan coast, sea-breeze boundaries can trigger afternoon cells that appear "out of nowhere"; inland desert showers are usually tied to passing upper-level disturbances.

## Limits to keep in mind

Radar sees precipitation in the air, not always what reaches the ground. Virga (rain that evaporates before landing) is common in dry Fezzan air. For totals and probability by hour, combine the radar view with the hourly chart on the city page.

## Quick checklist before you head out

- Check the **next 60 minutes** of nowcast for your location.
- Confirm the **probability and accumulation** in the hourly panel.
- In winter coastal storms, watch for repeated bands training over the same area — that is when local flooding risk rises.
